# Drama (ADA3M)

**Drama (ADA3M)** is a Grade 11 university-college preparation course in [The Arts](../subjects/the-arts.md).

This course requires students to create and perform in dramatic presentations. Students will analyse, interpret, and perform dramatic works from various cultures and time periods. Students will research various acting styles and conventions that could be  used in their presentations, and analyse the functions of playwrights, directors, actors, designers, technicians, and audiences.

## Before you take this course

Students should first complete one of [Drama (ADA1O)](ADA1O.md), or [Drama (ADA2O)](ADA2O.md) (any one satisfies the requirement).

## Where this course leads

Completing this course opens the door to [Drama (ADA4M)](ADA4M.md).

## What students learn

The learning is organized into 3 strands: Creating and Presenting, Reflecting, Responding, and Analysing, and Foundations.

### A. Creating and Presenting

*By the end of this course, students will:*

**A1 The Creative Process.** use the creative process and a variety of sources and forms, both individually and collaboratively, to design and develop [drama works](../glossary/drama-works.md)

- develop interpretations of drama texts or other sources from a variety of Western and non-Western traditions as a basis for their own drama presentations (e.g., interpretations of narratives by men, women, and children from diverse backgrounds; scenes from [Kabuki](../glossary/kabuki-theatre.md)or [Noh theatre](../glossary/noh-theatre.md); excerpts from Tennessee Williams’s The Glass Menagerie, Marsha Norman’s ’Night Mother, or Dennis Foon’s Skin; scenes from plays by men and women playwrights, including [Aboriginal,](../glossary/aboriginal-person-d00a2d62.md) Asian Canadian/American, or African Canadian/American dramatists)
- select and use appropriate drama forms to present a variety of adapted or original drama works (e.g., use a [drama anthology](../glossary/drama-anthology-fc43fa15.md) to present a famous person’s life story; use a skit with slapstick humour to comment on a recent media event; use puppetry to adapt a children’s book on a social or environmental theme, such as The Lorax \[Seuss\] or The Great Kapok Tree \[Cherry\], for a dramatic presentation)
- create and interpret a range of characters using a variety of acting approaches (e.g., present a scene in the style of melodrama or commedia dell’arte; use the Laban approach to create an original character)

<sub>The source curriculum adds 3 teacher prompts, examples, and instructional notes for these expectations.</sub>

**A2 Elements and Conventions.** use the elements and conventions of drama effectively in creating individual and ensemble drama works, including works based on a variety of sources

- highlight selected [elements of drama](../glossary/elements-of-drama-a43d2502.md) and subordinate others to achieve specific purposes (e.g., use setting and relationship to illuminate character; use time to explore relationship)
- use a variety of drama conventions to establish a distinctive [context](../glossary/context-for-a-work-of-art.md) or role in original or adapted works (e.g., use [guided imagery](../glossary/guided-imagery.md) and mapping to visualize settings and relationships; use [hot-seating](../glossary/hot-seating.md)or voices in the head to build a rounded picture of a character)

<sub>The source curriculum adds 2 teacher prompts, examples, and instructional notes for these expectations.</sub>

**A3 Presentation Techniques and Technologies.** use a variety of presentation techniques and technological tools to enhance the impact of drama works and communicate for specific audiences and purposes

- use a variety of techniques to increase interaction with or participation by the audience (e.g., use [forum theatre](../glossary/forum-theatre-fec6262d.md) to involve the whole class in developing a character or exploring a theme; situate some of the action in [non-stage areas](../glossary/stage-areas-ba58beca.md) of the theatre)
- use a range of techniques and acting approaches to refine performance during rehearsal (e.g., use vocal, movement, trust, and relaxation exercises to prepare for rehearsal; use hot-seating and [writing in role](../glossary/writing-in-role-c5641a8a.md) to extend character development  during rehearsal)
- select and use a variety of technological tools, including forms of new media, to highlight the message and enhance the impact of drama works (e.g., use traditional and digital music and video supports to create lighting and sound effects that enhance a specific mood)

*Transferable skills developed in this strand: Innovation, Creativity & Entrepreneurship, Critical Thinking & Problem Solving, Collaboration, Communication, Global Citizenship and Sustainability, Digital Literacy.*

### B. Reflecting, Responding, and Analysing

*By the end of this course, students will:*

**B1 The Critical Analysis Process.** use the critical analysis process to reflect on and evaluate their own and others’ drama works

- use the critical analysis process before and during drama projects to assign roles within the group, monitor the group process, and modify the roles and process as needed (e.g., use brainstorming and group discussion to generate ideas, assign roles, and agree on a process that includes a mechanism for reviewing progress and providing feedback; use [journal writing](../glossary/journal-writing-0694a20e.md) to reflect on progress during rehearsal; create and use a self- or peer-assessment tool based on student-developed criteria)
- analyse drama works to determine how they communicate ideas about issues, [culture](../glossary/culture-3a282604.md), and society (e.g., compare the different ways in which dramas on the same topic present their themes; compare the presentation of female characters by women playwrights in two different eras)
- analyse and evaluate the [aesthetic](../glossary/aesthetic.md) and technical aspects of drama works of diverse genres and styles (e.g., give feedback to peers about the strengths and areas for improvement of their directing decisions and stagecraft choices)

<sub>The source curriculum adds 3 teacher prompts, examples, and instructional notes for these expectations.</sub>

**B2 Drama and Society.** demonstrate an understanding of how societies present and past use or have used drama, and of how creating and viewing drama can benefit individuals, groups, and communities

- analyse different styles of drama and explain their influence on artistic and social conditions in diverse communities and cultures from the past and present (e.g., explain how the emerging style of naturalism in drama enabled Chekhov, Ibsen, or Shaw to address issues of his day, including themes of social change; explain the reasons for Shaw’s satirical attack on the well-made [play](../glossary/play.md); describe alienation in [Brechtian theatre](../glossary/brechtian-theatre.md)as a reaction to the elevation of theatrical illusion over message; assess the influence of groups such as Dreamrider Theatre and Ubom! Eastern Cape Drama Company in raising awareness of environmental issues)
- identify ways in which drama can influence personal growth, relationships with others, and aesthetic judgement (e.g., issue-based and whole-group drama activities can help develop [empathy](../glossary/empathy-empathize-e79a68a9.md), self-knowledge, and social and environmental awareness; participation in the creative process can develop skills in applying aesthetic criteria to improve or evaluate a final product)
- identify ways in which drama can influence the broader community (e.g., by giving expression to previously unheard voices; by highlighting systemic or emerging social  problems)
- explain how different types of theatre mirror cultural diversity and local or regional concerns in Canadian and global societies from the past and present (e.g., explain the link between the use of joual in Quebec theatre in the 1970s and the political unrest in the province at that time; after viewing a production or video of Theatre Passe Muraille’s Farm Show, explain the reasons for its documentary-style portrayal of people from a small rural community; explain how types or styles of theatre represented by Noh theatre, Famous People Players, Cirque de Soleil, Green Thumb theatre, or De-Ba-Jeh-Mu-Jig theatre reflect aspects of the communities that produced them)

<sub>The source curriculum adds 4 teacher prompts, examples, and instructional notes for these expectations.</sub>

**B3 Connections Beyond the Classroom.** identify knowledge and skills they have acquired through drama activities, and demonstrate an understanding of ways in which they can apply this learning in personal, social, and career contexts

- identify the collaborative skills and techniques they used to produce [ensemble](../glossary/ensemble.md)drama works, and explain how they can be applied in a variety of other contexts (e.g., explain the connection between carrying out an assigned role in staging a drama and contributing to a school committee or “playing their position” in a team sport)
- analyse their use of the creative process in drama activities, and explain what they learned from it and how that learning can be applied in work and other social contexts (e.g., how creative thinking might help them solve problems in other school subjects, based on their experience in drama work)
- relate the various functions they have performed in drama activities to educational and career opportunities in the broader educational and arts sectors (e.g., describe, in a  portfolio, how the skills acquired through involvement in community theatre can be applied elsewhere in the arts; identify postsecondary careers and university and college programs in the field of drama and theatre where they could apply their drama experience and skills)

### C. Foundations

*By the end of this course, students will:*

**C1 Concepts and Terminology.** demonstrate an understanding of the nature and functions of drama forms, elements, conventions, and techniques, including the correct terminology for the various components

- identify and describe the forms, elements, conventions, and techniques used in a variety of drama styles, and explain how they help achieve specific purposes and effects (e.g., explain how the use of formal postures and gestures supports the intended effect of drawing-room comedy; describe how tension is created in scenes by dramatists such as Molière, Henrik Ibsen, Thornton Wilder, Lillian Hellman, Carol Bolt, or Michel Tremblay)
- use correct terminology for the various components and processes of their own and others’ drama works (e.g., script analysis, character actor, supporting role, [dialogue](../glossary/dialogue-6ea18026.md), [role on the wall](../glossary/role-on-the-wall-9ae47edd.md), hot-seating, method acting, classical technique)
- demonstrate an understanding of production and promotion roles, practices, and terminology (e.g., set and costume design and construction processes; technical design and support roles; marketing and publicity tools and strategies)

<sub>The source curriculum adds 3 teacher prompts, examples, and instructional notes for these expectations.</sub>

**C2 Contexts and Influences.** demonstrate an understanding of the origins and development of drama and theatre arts and their influence on past and present societies

- locate, synthesize, and communicate information about scripts, performance spaces, and theatre traditions from different periods and cultures (e.g., create a comparison chart of conventions used in the staging of [Greek](../glossary/greek-theatre.md), Roman, and medieval theatre; describe the differences in make-up styles in Noh theatre, Renaissance drama, and other genres; highlight key elements of [Elizabethan](../glossary/elizabethan-theatre.md) and Restoration acting styles)
- compare the acting skill sets required by performers in current media to those required in traditional theatre (e.g., explain how the merits and limitations of television, the Internet, webcasts, and/or films shape the performances  of actors in these media)

<sub>The source curriculum adds 2 teacher prompts, examples, and instructional notes for these expectations.</sub>

**C3 Responsible Practices.** demonstrate an understanding of safe, ethical, and responsible personal and interpersonal practices in drama activities

- identify and follow safe and ethical practices in all drama activities (e.g., use vocal and physical warm-ups to protect against strain injuries; tape cords to ensure safety; store and handle flats safely; follow instructions and protocols for operating equipment; follow procedures for the environmentally responsible use of materials and [energy](../glossary/energy-7b16847d.md); honour the dramatist’s intentions; obtain performance rights; present and honour a variety of perspectives)
- demonstrate an understanding of the tasks and responsibilities involved in producing drama works (e.g., itemize the multiple and interrelated responsibilities and competencies of director, [stage manager](../glossary/stage-manager.md), costume manager, and writer)
- demonstrate an understanding of correct theatre worker and [audience etiquette](../glossary/audience-etiquette-2e173e54.md) in classroom drama work and formal performance contexts (e.g., as a director: show respect for actors’ opinions and approaches; as a performer: accept direction from peer coaches and teachers; respect the fourth wall; as a viewer: maintain a receptive attitude; respond courteously)

*Transferable skills developed in this strand: Critical Thinking & Problem Solving, Communication, Self-Directed Learning, Global Citizenship and Sustainability, Innovation, Creativity & Entrepreneurship.*
