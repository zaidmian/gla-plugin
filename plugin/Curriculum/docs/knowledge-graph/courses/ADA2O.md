# Drama (ADA2O)

**Drama (ADA2O)** is a Grade 10 open course in [The Arts](../subjects/the-arts.md).

This course provides opportunities for students to explore dramatic forms, conventions, and techniques. Students will explore a variety of dramatic sources from various cultures and representing a range of genres. Students will use the elements of drama in creating and communicating through dramatic works. Students will assume responsibility for decisions made in the creative and collaborative processes and will reflect on their experiences.

## Before you take this course

This course has no formal prerequisites.

## Where this course leads

Completing this course opens the door to [Drama (ADA3M)](ADA3M.md).

## What students learn

The learning is organized into 3 strands: Creating and Presenting, Reflecting, Responding, and Analysing, and Foundations.

### A. Creating and Presenting

*By the end of this course, students will:*

**A1 The Creative Process.** use the creative process and a variety of sources and forms, both individually and collaboratively, to design and develop [drama works](../glossary/drama-works.md)

- develop interpretations of issues from contemporary or historical sources (e.g., photographs, videos, music, newspaper/magazine articles, an eyewitness account of a historical event) as the basis for drama
- select and use appropriate forms to present identified issues from a variety of perspectives (e.g., use a radio [play](../glossary/play.md), [improvisation](../glossary/improvisation-97af099e.md), or series of tableaux to present two opposing views about a political, social, or environmental issue)
- use role play and characterization to explore personal and social issues (e.g., with a partner, create or assume a [role](../glossary/role.md) that explores an issue such as bullying; create a scenario that reveals details about a [character](../glossary/character-role.md)’s motivation)

<sub>The source curriculum adds 3 teacher prompts, examples, and instructional notes for these expectations.</sub>

**A2 Elements and Conventions.** use the elements and [conventions of drama](../glossary/conventions-of-drama.md) effectively in creating individual and [ensemble](../glossary/ensemble-9ff328fb.md) drama works, including works based on a variety of sources

- select and combine the elements of drama to achieve a variety of purposes in ensemble presentations (e.g., use the elements of character, [time, and place](../glossary/place-and-time.md) in a drama about making a difficult choice; use the elements of time and place to clarify the [focus](../glossary/focus-or-emphasis.md) in a drama about a historical event)
- use a variety of conventions to create a distinct voice that reflects a particular global, social, or personal perspective (e.g., use [voices in the head](../glossary/voices-in-the-head-005bc5db.md), [role on the wall](../glossary/role-on-the-wall-9ae47edd.md), and [hot seating](../glossary/hot-seating.md) to create a complex character from another region or country)

<sub>The source curriculum adds 2 teacher prompts, examples, and instructional notes for these expectations.</sub>

**A3 Presentation Techniques and Technologies.** use a variety of presentation techniques and technological tools to enhance the impact of drama works and communicate for specific audiences and purposes

- identify and use a variety of techniques to influence the audience in specific ways (e.g., have actors enter the [performance space](../glossary/performance-space.md) from the audience to increase audience connection to the drama; use [blocking](../glossary/blocking-9d85a40e.md) to focus audience attention on key characters or [relationships](../glossary/relationship-s.md) between characters)
- use a variety of voice and movement techniques to support the creation of character or [atmosphere](../glossary/atmosphere.md) during rehearsal (e.g., use voice and movement to suggest an airport, circus, or factory environment)
- use a variety of technological tools (e.g., light, sound, set design, props, models) to enhance the impact of drama works

*Transferable skills developed in this strand: Critical Thinking & Problem Solving, Communication, Collaboration, Global Citizenship and Sustainability, Digital Literacy, Innovation, Creativity & Entrepreneurship.*

### B. Reflecting, Responding, and Analysing

*By the end of this course, students will:*

**B1 The Critical Analysis Process.** use the critical analysis process to reflect on and evaluate their own and others’ drama works and activities

- use the critical analysis process before and during drama projects to identify and assess individual and peer roles and responsibilities in producing drama works (e.g., identify and assess the contribution of leadership and supporting roles, group dynamics, and cooperative problem solving to their process of creating drama works)
- analyse a variety of drama works to compare and assess how they explore universal themes and issues (e.g., compare and contrast the handling of similar themes in dramatizations of folk tales, myths, legends, personal stories, and/or Aboriginal tales)
- identify [aesthetic](../glossary/aesthetic.md) and technical aspects of drama works and explain how they help achieve specific dramatic purposes (e.g., write theatre or film reviews assessing whether the lighting, sound, set design, and costumes of a drama are used effectively to illustrate the intended message)

<sub>The source curriculum adds 3 teacher prompts, examples, and instructional notes for these expectations.</sub>

**B2 Drama and Society.** demonstrate an understanding of how societies present and past use or have used drama, and of how creating and viewing drama can benefit individuals, groups, and communities

- identify different types of drama and explain their function in diverse communities and cultures from the past and present (e.g., the function of television, film, or video game dramas with predictable [plot](../glossary/plot.md) lines and stock or stereotypical characters in today’s society; the function of theatre in ancient Greece, liturgical drama in medieval Europe, Shakespearean drama in Elizabethan England, and/or “social problem” dramas today)
- explain how [dramatic exploration](../glossary/dramatic-exploration-825fdaa7.md) helps develop awareness of different roles and identities people have in society (e.g., explain what they learned through [role playing](../glossary/role-playing-role-play-903d2c52.md) characters from different socio-economic groups)
- describe ways in which different types of dramatic exploration and drama presentations contribute to the school and broader community (e.g., list school productions, community theatre, professional theatre, street theatre, children’s theatre, or school-spirit assemblies they have attended, and describe how they and the audience benefited from the experience)
- identify ways in which dramatic exploration contributes to their understanding of diverse cultures and traditions (e.g., identify insights they gained through exploring the role of ritual in [Greek theatre](../glossary/greek-theatre.md) and/or Aboriginal ceremonies)

<sub>The source curriculum adds 4 teacher prompts, examples, and instructional notes for these expectations.</sub>

**B3 Connections Beyond the Classroom.** identify knowledge and skills they have acquired through drama activities and ways in which they can apply this learning in personal, social, and career contexts

- identify and describe skills, attitudes, and strategies they used in collaborative drama activities (e.g., brainstorming, [active listening](../glossary/active-listening-e80de31f.md), and cooperative problem-solving skills; strategies for sharing responsibility through collaborative team roles)
- identify skills they have developed through drama activities and explain how they can be useful in work and other social contexts (e.g., explain in a journal how their brainstorming and negotiation skills support teamwork in a variety of contexts)
- identify connections between their learning in drama and possible employment opportunities in the broader educational and arts sectors (e.g., production and/or performance roles in community theatre, television/radio broadcasting, filmmaking)

### C. Foundations

*By the end of this course, students will:*

**C1 Concepts and Terminology.** demonstrate an understanding of the nature and functions of drama forms, elements, conventions, and techniques, including the correct terminology for the various components

- identify the drama forms, elements, conventions, and techniques used in their own and others’ drama works, and explain how the various components are used, or can be used, to achieve specific effects, with a focus on ensemble drama works (e.g., how a comic drama [form](../glossary/form-80af155f.md) can be used to convey a serious message, how setting and time period can be used to sharpen the focus on a moral dilemma, how characters can be used to vary the [mood](../glossary/mood.md) within a drama)
- demonstrate an understanding of and use correct terminology to refer to the forms, elements, conventions, and techniques of drama, with a focus on ensemble drama works (e.g., [chorus](../glossary/chorus.md), [protagonist](../glossary/protagonist.md), ingénue, supporting role, act, scene, climax, resolution, improvisation, mask, [freeze-frame image](../glossary/freeze-frame-image-2a953857.md))
- demonstrate an understanding of production roles, practices, and terminology when planning and presenting drama works (e.g., set design, costume design, lighting plot, light [cue sheet](../glossary/cue-sheet.md), sound cue sheet, [prompt book](../glossary/prompt-book.md), set sketch, set model)

<sub>The source curriculum adds 3 teacher prompts, examples, and instructional notes for these expectations.</sub>

**C2 Contexts and Influences.** demonstrate an understanding of the origins and development of drama and theatre arts and their influence on past and present societies

- identify ways in which dramatic expression and performance reflect communities and cultures, past and present (e.g., the prominence of socially and/or politically powerful characters in the drama of pre-industrial societies; the use of boy actors for female roles in Shakespearean theatre; the [emphasis](../glossary/focus-or-emphasis.md) on religious themes in the drama of many cultures in different eras)
- describe how drama is used for various purposes in a range of social contexts (e.g., to express or celebrate group or community sentiments or values in street theatre or parades; to mark important historical or religious anniversaries of a country or culture; to raise awareness of social, environmental, and political issues; to explore personal relationships or social arrangements)

<sub>The source curriculum adds 2 teacher prompts, examples, and instructional notes for these expectations.</sub>

**C3 Responsible Practices.** demonstrate an understanding of safe, ethical, and responsible personal and interpersonal practices in drama activities

- identify and follow safe and ethical practices in drama activities (e.g., exhibit safe use of sound and lighting boards; follow procedures for the environmentally responsible use of materials and energy; prepare an individual or group seminar report on the nature and purpose of one or more of the following: copyright protection, royalties, public domain, intellectual property rights)
- identify and apply the skills and attitudes needed to perform various tasks and responsibilities in producing drama works (e.g., use active listening and cooperative problem-solving skills; practise punctuality; use tact in suggesting changes and improvements; demonstrate willingness to accept criticism and build consensus)
- demonstrate an understanding of theatre and audience etiquette, in both classroom and formal performance contexts (e.g., as a performer: show willingness to take direction and behave appropriately towards other actors; as a viewer: demonstrate respect for performers and other audience members by paying attention, not interrupting or talking, and applauding when appropriate)
