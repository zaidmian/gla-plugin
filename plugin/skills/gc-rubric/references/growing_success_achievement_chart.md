# Growing Success: what a rubric must follow

Source: *Growing Success: Assessment, Evaluation, and Reporting in Ontario Schools* (Ontario Ministry of Education, 2010), as summarised in `Teaching/Standards/assessment/growing-success-summary.md`. Page numbers refer to the 2010 PDF. This file carries the parts that govern rubric design so the skill can cite them inside the claude.ai sandbox; the summary in Standards is the fuller text.

## The achievement chart (Chapter 3, p. 15)

Every Ontario curriculum document contains an achievement chart, a province-wide performance standard organised into four categories of knowledge and skills, treated as interrelated:

- **Knowledge and Understanding**: subject-specific content acquired (knowledge) and the comprehension of its meaning and significance (understanding).
- **Thinking**: the use of critical and creative thinking skills and processes. The name of this category varies by subject; see below.
- **Communication**: the conveying of meaning through various forms.
- **Application**: the use of knowledge and skills to make connections within and between various contexts.

### Naming the second category

Three of the four names are identical in every Ontario curriculum document. The second is not, and the rubric must carry the name printed in the course's own curriculum document.

| Subject | Second category |
|---|---|
| Science, Grades 9 to 12 (SNC, SBI, SCH, SPH, SVN); Science and Technology, Grades 1 to 8 | **Thinking and Investigation** |
| **Computer Studies** (ICD2O, ICS3U, ICS4U, ICS3C, ICS4C) | **Thinking** |
| Mathematics, English, The Arts, Technological Education, Business Studies, and the rest | **Thinking** |

In Science the category takes in inquiry and research skills alongside critical and creative thinking, and its criteria begin with *initiating and planning*. Computer Studies is a separate subject with its own curriculum document, and both of those documents (2008 for ICS3U and ICS4U, 2023 for ICD2O) name the category **Thinking**, with the criteria use of planning skills, use of processing skills, use of critical/creative thinking processes.

One caveat, because it is where the mistake comes from: the ICD2O document's Descriptors paragraph reads "Effectiveness is the descriptor used for each criterion in the Thinking and Investigation, Communication, and Application categories". That sentence is boilerplate copied from the Science template and contradicts the achievement chart on the facing page. A Computer Studies rubric says **Thinking**.

Achievement is judged against **criteria** within each category, using **descriptors** expressed through qualifiers that map to the four levels: *limited* (Level 1), *some* (Level 2), *considerable* (Level 3), *a high degree of* or *thorough* (Level 4).

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Level 1 | Achievement falls much below the provincial standard, limited effectiveness |
| Level 2 | Achievement approaches the provincial standard, some effectiveness |
| **Level 3** | **Achievement at the provincial standard**, considerable effectiveness |
| Level 4 | Achievement surpasses the provincial standard, high degree of effectiveness |

**Level 3 is the provincial standard.** A Level 3 descriptor describes work that fully meets the expectation; Level 4 describes work that surpasses it, not merely Level 3 with an adverb. Achievement below Level 1 is recorded on Ontario report cards as "R" for Grades 1 to 8 and as a percentage below 50 for Grades 9 to 12 (Chapter 5); Google Classroom rubrics carry it as the "Below Level 1" row.

## Definitions the skill relies on (Glossary, p. 143)

- **Rubric**: "a scale that uses brief statements based on the criteria provided in the achievement chart ... to describe the levels of achievement of a process, product, or performance."
- **Success criteria**: descriptions of successful attainment of learning goals, based on achievement-chart criteria and agreed with students; they describe what success looks like.
- **Criterion-referenced assessment**: assessment against a predetermined standard or set of criteria, never against other students' performance. A rubric descriptor therefore never compares a student with classmates.
- **Curriculum expectations**: overall (by the end of the course) and specific (in greater detail). A rich performance task usually addresses all four categories and more than one overall expectation.
- **Modifications** versus **accommodations**: changes to the expectations versus changes to how a student accesses the task without altering the expectations. A rubric for an accommodated student is the same rubric; a rubric for modified expectations is a different rubric.

## Fundamental principles that shape wording (Chapter 1, p. 5)

Assessment must be fair, transparent, and equitable, planned to relate to the curriculum expectations and learning goals, and communicated clearly to students at the start. Descriptive feedback should be clear, specific, meaningful, and timely, and students should be able to assess their own work against the criteria. This is why descriptors are written to the student, in plain language, describing what the work does rather than labelling its quality.

## What stays out of a rubric (Chapter 2, p. 9)

The six learning skills and work habits (Responsibility, Organization, Independent Work, Collaboration, Initiative, Self-Regulation) are assessed and reported separately on the E, G, S, N scale and are not included in the determination of a student's grades, apart from any learning skill that is itself a curriculum expectation. Lateness, effort, neatness of behaviour, and attendance therefore never appear as rubric criteria or as reasons for a lower level. Where a task's expectation includes a process skill (for example collaborating in a group task in a course that expects it), assess the demonstrated skill, not the conduct.
